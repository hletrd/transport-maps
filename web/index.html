    Sources and method

    Times assume you leave when you choose: the first service is not waited for. A flight connection is charged the minimum connection time or the expected wait for the next departure, whichever is longer, capped at the cost of leaving the terminal and re-entering it — which is the disembark and check-in times for that airport’s size, so about 55 minutes at a small airport and an hour and forty at a large one; rail legs carry no wait. A ferry does: half the interval between sailings. That cap is a consequence of the graph, not a claim about airports: a connection can always be routed out through the terminal and back in, so no wait can cost more than that, and where the real wait is longer the figure is an underestimate.

    Door to door, not gate to gate. The time includes the trip to the airport, check-in and security, border control where one is crossed, and the trip from the arrival airport into the city. A flight of two hours commonly makes a journey of five.

    Address search and click-to-address send the typed text or the clicked coordinates to OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim service (no more than one request a second, and never as you type). Page views are counted by Google Analytics. Everything else is computed in the browser from files on this site. What each of those collects is set out under Privacy below.

    What this does not know

    The air model is a regression on 107,366 flight legs. Held out of its own fit, it is wrong by a mean of about 9 minutes on the flying part alone, and it runs a few minutes long between 400 and 800 km and about six minutes long between 3,000 and 6,000 km. Every figure on this page is printed to the minute; none of them is accurate to the minute.

    That fit is valid between 150 and 6,000 km. Beyond 6,000 km it is extrapolation — receiver coverage over oceans is too sparse to measure a transpacific flight end to end — so the intercontinental readings, the ones a visitor is most likely to look up, are the least certain on the map. Long-haul flying also varies by about an hour on winds alone, which a distance-only model cannot represent. Treat anything over 10,000 km as approximate.

    Rail. Running time comes from one of six service tiers, 74 km/h for a commuter service up to 215 km/h for a high-speed one, with a separate 20 km/h tier for heritage and tourist lines, all fitted against journey times recorded in OpenStreetMap. A route’s tier is read from its own service tag where it carries one — about three in four do; the rest fall to a general-purpose default, which will be too slow for an express and too fast for a stopping service. The largest error this model is known to make is on the Tohoku Shinkansen, where it returns about 10 hours against a published 3 h 10, and the larger part of that is speed. All three Shinkansen relations in the data carry no service tag at all, so all three are priced at the default, 82 km/h; the tag that marks the line as high-speed sits on the individual track segments rather than on the route, so the route reads as ordinary. Pricing them as high-speed instead would take about 5 hours off, 70% of the error. The stop sequence is the second and smaller cause: the relation lists 21 station calls where the fastest train makes about six, and the per-stop allowance for the other fifteen is 37 minutes, 9% of the error. Corrected both ways the journey would still come out near 4 hours, so a better tag on its own would not reach the published time. Long-distance express services on all-stations relations are over-stated for the same reasons, anywhere in the world.

    Flight frequency is a gravity model fitted to two anchor routes, not a timetable. Rail legs carry no waiting time at all. A ferry is charged half the interval between sailings, taken from the route’s own OpenStreetMap timetable where it has one and otherwise from a headway fitted to eight published crossings — a wide prior, about a factor of two out at one sigma against those crossings and a factor of 3.6 against 326 other timetables in the data. The bias changes sign at about 150 km: below it the prior claims more frequent service than reality and under-states the wait (median predicted over observed 0.21 to 0.58 across 298 of those 326 crossings), and above it, over-states (2.21 at 150–400 km, 1.68 at 400–1,000 km). Distance alone cannot tell a remote lifeline route from a busy short-sea one. A build made before that model charges no ferry wait at all; the ferry line in the itinerary is generated by the build itself, so it always describes the one being served. Ground speeds describe a road class, not a road. Four of the six classes are fitted to 2,998 driving journeys sampled from the Google Routes API — the one commercial service this project uses, and it is used during calibration only: nothing from it reaches this site, and no commercial flight data is used anywhere. Roadless terrain and local roads keep published-figure defaults, because the fit wanted roadless terrain infinitely fast and drew local roads from 116 km of driving across four journeys. Roadless terrain is the slowest of the six classes and local roads the third, so between them they set the last kilometres of many journeys. And the build that is being served may lag the code: index.json records what was actually computed.

    Long over-water flights. Every flight here is a scheduled service, already operating under its airline’s extended-range approval — ETOPS, and its equivalents for three and four engines. So an ETOPS rule is not a constraint this map could apply on top of the routes: it is a constraint the routes were already flying under before they reached the data. That is why there is no setting for it, and why adding one would be a switch that changed no figure on this page. The more useful admission is the second one: this model could not represent the difference if there were one. A flight’s time here is a function of the great-circle distance between its two airports and nothing else. The data carries no aircraft type and no engine count, so there is nothing to apply a rule to; and the page draws the great circle, which is precisely the track an ETOPS-limited flight does not fly. The real cost of staying inside a diversion radius is a longer track, and a longer track is the one thing this model has no way to express.

    Where the number is read. The outlined hexagon under the pointer is the cell the time is read from: resolution 6, about 6.5 km across and 36 km². Where the road network is dense the bands are painted one level finer, at 2.4 km, so in those places the colour under the pointer distinguishes ground the reading does not: the number is the value of that cell's centre sub-cell, not an average over the seven. The breakdown beneath the number — the arrival airport, the mode of the last leg — comes from a wider grid still, about 45 km across, and can name a different airport from the one the time was computed through. Until the finer array has loaded, or if your browser is set to save data, the number itself comes from that wider grid and the line under it says so.

    Privacy

    This site has no accounts, no sign-in and no advertising, and nothing collected here is sold or shared beyond the two services named below.

    The link in the address bar. Choosing a destination writes it into the address bar, to about a metre, so the journey can be shared or reloaded. That address is part of what the analytics tag below records as the page location. Two things are deliberately kept out of it: a street address returned by reverse geocoding is never written there, so it is never reported as the page location — only a place name from this site’s own gazetteer is — and a clicked point is rounded to about 110 m before it is sent to Nominatim.

    Google Analytics. Page views are counted by Google Analytics, which sets cookies and identifiers in your browser and sends Google your approximate location (derived from your IP address), your browser and device type, the pages you open and how you arrived. How Google uses that data is described at google.com/policies/privacy/partners. Browser settings, an ad-blocker or Google’s own opt-out add-on will stop it.

    OpenStreetMap Nominatim. Searching an address, or asking for the name of a place you clicked, sends that text or those coordinates to Nominatim, run by the OpenStreetMap Foundation, along with the usual request metadata. It is never sent as you type. Turning off “Name the place under the cursor” in Settings stops the second kind entirely.

    What stays on your device. “Show my location” asks the browser for your position. The position itself is used only in the page, to draw a dot and work out which departure city is nearest, and is never sent to this site or to anyone else. The city it picks does leave, though, in the ordinary way: choosing a departure writes its name into the address bar, and the address bar is what the analytics tag records as the page location. So the reading is your nearest listed city, not where you are. Your colour scheme, your ocean colour, the two Settings switches and the carry-on choice are kept in this browser’s local storage and go no further. Everything else the map does is computed in your browser from files on this site.
