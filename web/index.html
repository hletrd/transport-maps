    Sources and method

    Times assume you leave when you choose: the first service is not waited for. A flight connection is charged the minimum connection time or the expected wait for the next departure, whichever is longer, capped at the cost of leaving the terminal and re-entering it — which is the disembark and check-in times for that airport’s size, so about 55 minutes at a small airport and an hour and forty at a large one; rail and ferry legs carry no wait. That cap is a consequence of the graph, not a claim about airports: a connection can always be routed out through the terminal and back in, so no wait can cost more than that, and where the real wait is longer the figure is an underestimate.

    Door to door, not gate to gate. The time includes the trip to the airport, check-in and security, border control where one is crossed, and the trip from the arrival airport into the city. A flight of two hours commonly makes a journey of five.

    Address search and click-to-address send the typed text or the clicked coordinates to OpenStreetMap’s Nominatim service (no more than one request a second, and never as you type). Page views are counted by Google Analytics. Everything else is computed in the browser from files on this site. What each of those collects is set out under Privacy below.

    What this does not know

    The air model is a regression on 107,366 flight legs. Held out of its own fit, it is wrong by a mean of about 9 minutes on the flying part alone, and it runs a few minutes long between 400 and 800 km and about six minutes long between 3,000 and 6,000 km. Every figure on this page is printed to the minute; none of them is accurate to the minute.

    That fit is valid between 150 and 6,000 km. Beyond 6,000 km it is extrapolation — receiver coverage over oceans is too sparse to measure a transpacific flight end to end — so the intercontinental readings, the ones a visitor is most likely to look up, are the least certain on the map. Long-haul flying also varies by about an hour on winds alone, which a distance-only model cannot represent. Treat anything over 10,000 km as approximate.

    Flight frequency is a gravity model fitted to two anchor routes, not a timetable. Rail and ferry legs carry no waiting time at all. Ground speeds describe a road class, not a road. Four of the six classes are fitted to 2,998 sampled driving journeys; roadless terrain and local roads keep published-figure defaults, because the fit wanted roadless terrain infinitely fast and drew local roads from 116 km of driving across four journeys. Those two are the slowest and the second slowest classes, so they set the last kilometres of many journeys. And the build that is being served may lag the code: index.json records what was actually computed.

    Where the number is read. The outlined hexagon under the pointer is the cell the time is read from: resolution 6, about 6.5 km across and 36 km². Where the road network is dense the bands are painted one level finer, at 2.4 km, so in those places the colour under the pointer distinguishes ground the reading averages over. The breakdown beneath the number — the arrival airport, the mode of the last leg — comes from a wider grid still, about 45 km across, and can name a different airport from the one the time was computed through. Until the finer array has loaded, or if your browser is set to save data, the number itself comes from that wider grid and the line under it says so.

    The map is drawn by four open-source libraries — MapLibre GL JS and PMTiles under BSD-3-Clause, h3-js under Apache-2.0 and fflate under MIT — set in IBM Plex Sans under the SIL Open Font License. Their copyright notices and licence texts are served with them.

    Privacy

    This site has no accounts, no sign-in and no advertising, and nothing collected here is sold or shared beyond the two services named below.

    The link in the address bar. Choosing a destination writes it into the address bar, to about a metre, so the journey can be shared or reloaded. That address is part of what the analytics tag below records as the page location. Reverse geocoding is deliberately coarser: a clicked point is rounded to about 110 m before it is sent to Nominatim.

    Google Analytics. Page views are counted by Google Analytics, which sets cookies and identifiers in your browser and sends Google your approximate location (derived from your IP address), your browser and device type, the pages you open and how you arrived. How Google uses that data is described at google.com/policies/privacy/partners. Browser settings, an ad-blocker or Google’s own opt-out add-on will stop it.

    OpenStreetMap Nominatim. Searching an address, or asking for the name of a place you clicked, sends that text or those coordinates to Nominatim, run by the OpenStreetMap Foundation, along with the usual request metadata. It is never sent as you type. Turning off “Name the place under the cursor” in Settings stops the second kind entirely.

    What stays on your device. “Show my location” asks the browser for your position. The position itself is used only in the page, to draw a dot and work out which departure city is nearest, and is never sent to this site or to anyone else. The city it picks does leave, though, in the ordinary way: choosing a departure writes its name into the address bar, and the address bar is what the analytics tag records as the page location. So the reading is your nearest listed city, not where you are. Your colour scheme, your ocean colour and the two Settings switches are kept in this browser’s local storage and go no further. Everything else the map does is computed in your browser from files on this site.
